## 1. THC-ENC-319 — Disease Resistance and Susceptibility

- Family: `diagnostic-decision-tree`
- Priority: **24**
- Purpose: Teach the learner to interpret disease resistance, tolerance, and susceptibility as genotype-by-pathogen-by-environment traits; use replicated Cannabis evidence without converting one resistant accession, locus, or trial into a universal cultivar claim.
- Required labels: resistance, susceptibility, tolerance, quantitative resistance, genotype-by-environment interaction
- Accuracy requirements: Cannabis genotypes vary substantially in disease susceptibility. Direct screening has documented large differences in powdery-mildew severity among Cannabis sativa germplasm, including entries with consistently low disease in replicated trials, and a 2026 Septoria study likewise found substantial susceptibility variation among accessions. | Resistance is pathogen specific. A genotype with low powdery-mildew severity should not be assumed resistant to Septoria, Botrytis, Fusarium, HLVd, BCTV, or another disease unless that interaction has been tested. | Genetic studies have identified Cannabis loci associated with powdery-mildew susceptibility or resistance, including evidence near CsMLO1. A candidate locus can strengthen biological understanding but does not make phenotype independent of genetic background, pathogen population, environment, or experimental design.
- Misconception guards: A cultivar resistant to one disease is broadly disease-resistant. | No visible disease in one crop cycle proves genetic resistance.

## 2. THC-ENC-320 — Disease Incident Records and Eradication Review

- Family: `diagnostic-decision-tree`
- Priority: **24**
- Purpose: Teach the learner to document a Cannabis disease incident from first signal through diagnosis, containment, source/pathway analysis, corrective action, verification, and closure so conclusions remain traceable and future crop cycles can test whether the problem truly recurred or was resolved.
- Required labels: incident record, case definition, containment, corrective action, verification of effectiveness
- Accuracy requirements: Disease incidents are easier to interpret when the evidence sequence is preserved: first symptom or positive test, case definition, plant and facility map, samples and results, suspected pathways, interventions, new cases over time, and final verification. Retrospective memory is much weaker than contemporaneous records. | Accurate diagnosis is central to integrated disease management. Cannabis greenhouse guidance recommends identifying disease hot spots, recording affected plant locations, and using diagnostic testing when the causal pathogen is unclear. These records allow later comparison among crop cycles and can reveal recurring sources or seasonal patterns. | Eradication is a strong claim. Removing symptomatic plants or obtaining one negative environmental sample does not prove an infectious agent has been eliminated from stock plants, propagation cohorts, water systems, media, debris, tools, facility niches, or asymptomatic hosts. Closure criteria should therefore be defined before the incident is closed.
- Misconception guards: Removing all visibly sick plants proves the pathogen is eradicated. | The first positive plant must be the source of the outbreak.

## 3. THC-ENC-321 — IPM Principles and the Prevention Hierarchy

- Family: `diagnostic-decision-tree`
- Priority: **24**
- Purpose: Teach the learner to explain integrated pest management as a recurring evidence-based decision process that prioritizes prevention, monitoring, correct identification, context-specific action thresholds, lower-risk interventions, and verification rather than routine calendar pesticide use.
- Required labels: integrated pest management, prevention hierarchy, action threshold, monitoring, verification
- Accuracy requirements: IPM is a process rather than a single control method. EPA describes a four-part decision sequence built around action thresholds, monitoring and accurate identification, prevention, and control selected for effectiveness and risk. | Prevention is the first line of defense because keeping pests and pathogens out, removing reservoirs, using clean plant material, and reducing favorable conditions can prevent a population or epidemic from becoming established. | Monitoring and identification come before intervention because not every organism is harmful and because injury, symptoms, pest abundance, crop stage, beneficial organisms, and environmental context affect the appropriate response.
- Misconception guards: IPM means never using pesticides. | IPM means spraying several products in rotation.

## 8. THC-ENC-326 — Cleaning Versus Disinfection

- Family: `diagnostic-decision-tree`
- Priority: **24**
- Purpose: Teach the learner to separate physical removal of contamination from microbial inactivation so sanitation procedures are designed as validated sequences rather than treating a disinfectant application as a substitute for cleaning.
- Required labels: cleaning, disinfection, sanitation, organic matter, sterilization
- Accuracy requirements: Cleaning and disinfection solve different problems. Cleaning removes material from a surface; disinfection attempts to inactivate susceptible microorganisms that remain under the conditions supported by the procedure or product label. | Greenhouse extension guidance consistently places cleaning before disinfection because residual soil, media, plant debris, sap, algae, and other organic matter can shield organisms or reduce the effectiveness of several disinfectants. | A visibly clean surface is not necessarily microbiologically clean, while a disinfectant applied to a visibly dirty surface has not necessarily reached or inactivated the target organism.
- Misconception guards: Spraying disinfectant on dirt is the same as cleaning and disinfecting. | A surface that looks clean is sterile.
