## 1. THC-ENC-087 — Leaf Temperature Measurement

- Family: `mechanism-process-diagram`
- Priority: **24**
- Purpose: Teach the learner to collect defensible leaf-temperature data with infrared or contact methods and avoid common targeting, emissivity, and reflection errors.
- Required labels: Infrared thermometry, Emissivity, Distance-to-spot ratio, Field of view
- Accuracy requirements: Infrared instruments measure radiation from a field of view, not an invisible point. If the spot includes background, pot, lamp, wall, or multiple leaves, the result is a mixed surface temperature. The leaf must fill the field of view, and the instrument's distance-to-spot specification must be respected. | Healthy plant leaves generally have high infrared emissivity, but instrument setting, surface wetness, angle, reflections, sensor-body temperature, and protective windows can affect readings. Contact thermocouples provide an alternative check but can shade, compress, or alter the leaf boundary layer. Method comparison is valuable during setup. | Biological sampling matters as much as instrument precision. Leaf temperature changes with height, orientation, radiation, transpiration, age, water status, and airflow. A repeatable protocol should specify leaf class, canopy zone, time relative to lights and irrigation, and whether a single-leaf or canopy-average method is intended.
- Misconception guards: The laser dot is the measurement spot: The dot is an aiming aid; the actual field of view is usually larger. | An IR gun measures internal tissue temperature: It estimates radiometric surface temperature.
- Sources: Fluke Corporation. 566/568 Infrared Thermometers Users Manual. | Apogee Instruments. SI-100 Series Infrared Radiometer Owner's Manual. | Nobel P.S. (2009). Physicochemical and Environmental Plant Physiology, 4th ed. Academic Press. | THC Cultivation SOP Source Materials Packet v1.0 (project source, May 2026).

## 2. THC-ENC-090 — Root-Zone Moisture and Plant Water Status

- Family: `diagnostic-decision-tree`
- Priority: **24**
- Purpose: Teach the learner to relate substrate water content, matric potential, salinity, root oxygen, and plant demand without treating one moisture reading as a complete diagnosis.
- Required labels: Volumetric water content, Matric potential, Container capacity, Plant-available water
- Accuracy requirements: Substrate water status can be described by amount, energy, or mass. Volumetric water content estimates how much water is present; matric potential estimates how tightly it is held; pot mass tracks total water change when plant and container mass are accounted for. These methods answer related but different questions. | The same water content does not mean the same availability in peat, coco, mineral soil, compost mixes, or rockwool. Bulk density, porosity, particle size, shrinkage, root occupancy, salinity, temperature, and sensor contact change the reading and plant response. Electromagnetic sensors require substrate-specific calibration for defensible absolute values. | Plant water status integrates root-zone supply with demand. A wet reading can coexist with wilting when roots are hypoxic, diseased, cold, saline, recently transplanted, or physically disconnected from the measured zone. A dry reading at one probe can coexist with adequate water elsewhere when irrigation distribution or root placement is uneven.
- Misconception guards: A moisture percentage is universal: The number depends on substrate, calibration, placement, temperature, and salinity. | Wet roots cannot wilt: Hypoxia, disease, salinity, cold, or hydraulic damage can restrict uptake.

## 3. THC-ENC-092 — Dryback: Measurement, Interpretation, and Limits

- Family: `mechanism-process-diagram`
- Priority: **24**
- Purpose: Teach the learner to define dryback with explicit units and reference points, then interpret the change without turning an industry shorthand into a universal plant target.
- Required labels: Dryback, Absolute VWC change, Relative change, Overnight dryback
- Accuracy requirements: Dryback is not one standardized number. It may mean loss of container mass, decline in VWC percentage points, relative decline from post-irrigation peak, or movement in matric potential. A report of '20% dryback' is uninterpretable unless the numerator, denominator, units, sensor, start time, and end time are stated. | The water-content curve reflects plant uptake, surface evaporation, drainage, redistribution, substrate shrinkage, sensor location, and temperature or EC effects on the probe. Early post-irrigation decline may include free drainage, while later decline may more closely reflect crop water use. Treating both segments as one physiological response can mislead. | Larger drybacks may increase air exposure in some substrates but can also increase osmotic concentration, reduce hydraulic conductivity, and produce stress. Very small drybacks can reflect frequent irrigation, low demand, oversized containers, poor drainage, or sensor placement. Neither direction is inherently generative or reproductive.
- Misconception guards: Dryback is always a percentage: It may be mass, VWC percentage points, relative VWC, or matric potential. | A larger dryback automatically improves quality: Stress effects are intensity-, duration-, stage-, and genotype-dependent.

## 4. THC-ENC-093 — Runoff, Leaching Fraction, and Salt Movement

- Family: `diagnostic-decision-tree`
- Priority: **24**
- Purpose: Teach the learner to measure drainage and leaching consistently and interpret runoff EC as a method-dependent indicator rather than a direct sample of the entire root zone.
- Required labels: Drainage, Leaching fraction, Electrical conductivity, Preferential flow
- Accuracy requirements: Leaching moves soluble ions downward when enough solution passes through the occupied root zone. A simple event leaching fraction is drainage volume divided by applied volume, but interpretation requires a defined collection period and correction for solution already in saucers, channels, or recirculation plumbing. | Drainage composition is shaped by input EC, resident pore solution, evapoconcentration, ion uptake, exchange reactions, dissolution, channeling, and sampling time. The first drainage may be concentrated or may follow a preferential pathway. A grab sample is not automatically the average root-zone solution. | More runoff is not automatically better. Excess drainage wastes water and nutrients, increases disposal load, can spread pathogens in shared systems, and may not correct poorly wetted pockets. Too little leaching can permit salts to accumulate in systems where ions are added faster than plants remove them.
- Misconception guards: Runoff EC equals root-zone EC: It is a method- and pathway-dependent sample. | A fixed runoff percentage fits every system: Substrate, salinity, water quality, recirculation, and environmental rules differ.
- Sources: Virginia Cooperative Extension. Leaching Fraction: A Tool to Schedule Irrigation for Container-Grown Nursery Crops. | Oregon State University Extension (2024). ## 5. THC-ENC-094 — Drought Stress and Recovery

- Family: `diagnostic-decision-tree`
- Priority: **24**
- Purpose: Teach the learner to recognize progressive water-deficit responses, distinguish reversible regulation from injury, and evaluate recovery with plant and root-zone measurements.
- Required labels: Water deficit, Turgor loss, Hydraulic failure, Recovery
- Accuracy requirements: As water availability declines, cells lose turgor, leaf expansion slows, abscisic-acid signaling increases, and stomata tend to close. Photosynthesis and transpiration decline, while leaf temperature may rise because evaporative cooling is reduced. Prolonged or severe stress can impair membranes, enzymes, roots, and vascular function. | Visible wilting is a late and nonspecific signal. High EC, root disease, hypoxia, heat load, transplant damage, or a blocked emitter can produce similar symptoms. Drought diagnosis requires evidence that substrate supply or hydraulic continuity was insufficient under the measured demand. | Rewatering can restore turgor quickly while photosynthesis, stomatal behavior, root function, or growth remain impaired. Repeated wilt-rewater cycles may select for smaller canopies or change development. Recovery must be measured after minutes, hours, and days rather than declared when leaves lift.
- Misconception guards: Wilting always means dry substrate: Root failure, salinity, disease, heat, or hypoxia can limit water uptake. | Leaves standing up proves full recovery: Gas exchange and growth can remain depressed after turgor returns.
- Sources: Caplan D., Dixon M., and Zheng Y. (2019). Increasing Inflorescence Dry Weight and Cannabinoid Content in Medical Cannabis Using Controlled Drought Stress. ## 6. THC-ENC-095 — Overwatering, Hypoxia, and Root Decline

- Family: `diagnostic-decision-tree`
- Priority: **24**
- Purpose: Teach the learner to explain how excessive water-filled pore space and poor drainage restrict root respiration and increase the risk of secondary root decline.
- Required labels: Hypoxia, Anoxia, Water-filled pore space, Anaerobic metabolism
- Accuracy requirements: Roots require oxygen for mitochondrial respiration, active transport, maintenance, growth, and defense. Gas diffusion through water-filled pores is far slower than through air-filled pores, so saturation, compaction, perched water, blocked drains, warm solution, and high root respiration can deplete oxygen near roots. | Hypoxic roots lose energy for membrane transport and ion regulation. Water and nutrient uptake can decline even though the substrate is wet. Roots may discolor, lose fine branches, shed cells, and produce abnormal odors, but color and odor alone do not identify a pathogen or prove oxygen concentration. | Overwet conditions can favor root-pathogen complexes by weakening host tissue and changing the rhizosphere. Pythium-like organisms, Fusarium species, and other agents require correct diagnosis. Hypoxia is an abiotic condition; it is not itself a fungal disease, although both can coexist.
- Misconception guards: Overwatering is simply too many milliliters: Frequency, drainage, pore structure, root demand, and saturation duration determine the oxygen result. | Wet plants cannot be thirsty: Hypoxic or diseased roots may fail to supply the shoot.
- Sources: Drew M.C. (1997). Oxygen Deficiency and Root Metabolism: Injury and Acclimation Under Hypoxia and Anoxia. ## 7. THC-ENC-097 — Cold Stress and Chilling Injury

- Family: `diagnostic-decision-tree`
- Priority: **24**
- Purpose: Teach the learner to identify how low tissue and root-zone temperatures alter membranes, metabolism, photosynthesis, water transport, and recovery in cannabis.
- Required labels: Chilling stress, Freezing injury, Photoinhibition, Cold acclimation
- Accuracy requirements: Cold slows enzyme activity, membrane transport, respiration, root growth, and water movement. Leaves may remain exposed to substantial light while carbon metabolism and repair slow, increasing photoinhibition risk. Chilled roots can restrict uptake even when air conditions appear acceptable. | Membrane lipids, genotype, prior acclimation, tissue age, duration, and rate of cooling influence injury. Symptoms can include slowed growth, drooping, discoloration, marginal injury, delayed rooting, and nutrient-like patterns. These signs are not unique and may appear after the cold event. | Night-time radiation, cold supply air, exterior surfaces, irrigation solution, outdoor transplant shock, and power failures can chill tissues below the room average. ## 8. THC-ENC-099 — Sensor Placement, Mapping, and Calibration

- Family: `diagnostic-decision-tree`
- Priority: **24**
- Purpose: Teach the learner to build a traceable environmental measurement system that distinguishes representative control sensors, diagnostic mapping instruments, and independent safety devices.
- Required labels: Calibration, Verification, Control sensor, Measurement uncertainty
- Accuracy requirements: A sensor measures its own location and exposure. Direct fixture radiation, cold walls, supply jets, humidifier plumes, wet leaves, irrigation splash, body heat, and poor shielding can bias temperature, RH, CO2, and leaf-temperature readings. Representative placement is a design decision, not whatever wall position is convenient. | Control and mapping require different architectures. A stable shielded canopy-level control sensor may drive HVAC, while a grid of portable or fixed sensors reveals spatial distribution. Independent safety sensors should not be treated as interchangeable with plant-control sensors, especially for CO2. | Calibration is more than pressing a reset button. It requires an appropriate reference, stabilization time, defined points across the operating range, as-found and as-left results, tolerance, adjustment authority, and traceability. RH sensors can drift or become contaminated; substrate probes require medium-specific validation; CO2 sensors may show age, cross-sensitivity, or placement bias.
- Misconception guards: A new sensor is calibrated for this room: Factory specification does not prove field placement or current agreement with a reference. | Two sensors that disagree can be averaged: The cause must be investigated before combining biased data.
- Sources: University of Massachusetts Extension (2016). Managing Light, Temperature, and Relative Humidity in Greenhouses. | Sui R. and Baggard J. (2015). ## 9. THC-ENC-101 — Light as Photons: PAR and ePAR

- Family: `mechanism-process-diagram`
- Priority: **24**
- Purpose: Teach the learner to distinguish photon-based plant-light measurements from human-vision and energy measurements, and state the wavelength band used for every value.
- Required labels: Photon, PAR, ePAR, PFD
- Accuracy requirements: Plants intercept photons, not lumens. Lumens weight radiation according to human visual sensitivity, while photosynthetic photon measurements count photons within a declared waveband. Irradiance in watts per square metre describes radiant energy, not photon number; the same radiant energy contains more red photons than blue photons because longer-wavelength photons carry less energy individually. | PAR is a measurement convention, not a substance emitted by a lamp. A conventional quantum sensor integrates photons from 400 to 700 nm and reports PPFD. Research showing that far-red photons can contribute to photosynthesis when combined with shorter wavelengths led to the proposed 400-750 nm ePAR range. PAR-PPFD and ePAR-ePPFD values are therefore not interchangeable. | A spectral photon distribution is the most complete description because it shows photon delivery by wavelength. A single integrated number cannot reveal blue fraction, green fraction, red peaks, far-red content, ultraviolet output, or sensor mismatch. Every record must pair the numerical value with the band, sensor model, calibration basis, location, and operating state.
- Misconception guards: PAR is light plants use equally: PAR defines a counting band; absorption, photosynthetic efficiency, signaling, and penetration still vary with wavelength and context. | Lux converts reliably to PPFD: Conversion depends strongly on the source spectrum and meter response; a generic factor can be seriously wrong.
- Sources: Apogee Instruments (2021). The New 400-750 nm ePAR Range Explained. | Rodriguez-Morrison V., Llewellyn D., and Zheng Y. (2021). Cannabis Yield, Potency, and Leaf Photosynthesis Respond Differently to Increasing Light Levels in an Indoor Environment. Frontiers in Plant Science 12:646020. | THC Cannabis Plant Science Source Packet v1.1 (project source, May 2026). | THC Cultivation SOP Source Materials Packet v1.0 (project source, May 2026).

## 10. THC-ENC-102 — PPF, PPFD, and Fixture Output

- Family: `measurement-workflow`
- Priority: **24**
- Purpose: Teach the learner to separate total fixture photon output from photon density at the crop and use each metric for the decision it actually supports.
- Required labels: PPF, PPFD, Photon efficacy, System efficiency
- Accuracy requirements: PPF describes total photon output from a source; PPFD describes spatial density at a receiving surface. A high-PPF fixture can still produce poor canopy PPFD if mounted too high, aimed badly, blocked, used without suitable overlap, or placed in a reflective geometry different from its test condition. | Photon efficacy is an equipment conversion metric, not crop productivity. It does not include fixture placement, room interception, spectrum-dependent response, canopy architecture, photoperiod, dimming losses, heat-removal energy, or yield quality. Wall-plug power must be measured under the actual operating state when energy calculations matter. | A fixture specification is only comparable when test method, input voltage, thermal state, spectrum, dimming level, and measurement uncertainty are known. Crop design must use a measured canopy map after installation; manufacturer PPF and distribution files are inputs to design, not proof of delivered photons.
- Misconception guards: More PPF guarantees more crop light: Room geometry and interception determine how much output reaches useful plant surfaces. | Micromoles per joule predicts yield: It describes photon conversion at the fixture, not biological or economic conversion by the crop system.
- Sources: Resource Innovation Institute (2021). LED Lighting for Cannabis Cultivation and Controlled Environment Agriculture: Best Practices Guide. | Apogee Instruments (2021). The New 400-750 nm ePAR Range Explained. | THC Cultivation SOP Source Materials Packet v1.0 (project source, May 2026).

- Family: `diagnostic-decision-tree`
- Priority: **24**
- Purpose: Teach the learner to design a repeatable canopy-light measurement procedure that controls sensor choice, orientation, geometry, timing, and canopy condition.
- Required labels: Quantum sensor, Cosine response, Spectral mismatch, Measurement plane
- Accuracy requirements: A canopy measurement is a method, not just a meter reading. Select a sensor whose wavelength response matches the metric and source. Level it horizontally for downwelling top light unless the protocol intentionally measures another direction. ## 12. THC-ENC-107 — Inverse Square Effects and Fixture Geometry

- Family: `measurement-workflow`
- Priority: **24**
- Purpose: Teach the learner to use inverse-square reasoning only where its assumptions apply and explain how extended fixtures, overlap, reflectance, and canopy geometry alter distance effects.
- Required labels: Inverse-square law, Point source, Beam distribution, Near field
- Accuracy requirements: The inverse-square law is exact for an ideal point source radiating into open space. Horticultural bars, panels, reflectors, lenses, multiple fixtures, walls, and nearby canopies violate that simplified geometry. At distances comparable with fixture dimensions, different parts of an extended source are at different distances and angles from the sensor. | Lowering a fixture generally increases local intensity and reduces the footprint, often worsening hotspots unless overlap and dimming are adjusted. Raising it can improve overlap but increases spill and interception by non-crop surfaces. Reflective boundaries return some photons, while dust, ageing, and dark surfaces change the result. | Geometry should be solved with photometric or ray-tracing information when available and verified by a measured grid. The inverse-square relationship is useful for direction and hazard awareness, but a ratio calculation is not a substitute for an installed map.
- Misconception guards: Double distance always quarters PPFD: That rule assumes a point source in open space and usually fails for close extended arrays and overlapping fixtures. | Lower is always more efficient: Lowering can create unusable hotspots, poor overlap, access problems, and stress.
- Sources: Resource Innovation Institute (2021). LED Lighting for Cannabis Cultivation and Controlled Environment Agriculture: Best Practices Guide. | THC Cultivation SOP Source Materials Packet v1.0 (project source, May 2026). | THC Cannabis Plant Science Source Packet v1.1 (project source, May 2026).

## 15. THC-ENC-111 — Photoinhibition and High-Light Stress

- Family: `diagnostic-decision-tree`
- Priority: **24**
- Purpose: Teach the learner to distinguish regulated photoprotection from sustained photoinhibition and diagnose high-light injury using plant, environmental, and spatial evidence.
- Required labels: Photoinhibition, NPQ, Photobleaching, Fv/Fm
- Accuracy requirements: Plants routinely protect photosystems by changing leaf angle, closing stomata, dissipating excitation energy, moving chloroplasts, producing protective pigments, and repairing photosystem components. ## 16. THC-ENC-115 — Ultraviolet Radiation: Evidence and Safety

- Family: `diagnostic-decision-tree`
- Priority: **24**
- Purpose: Teach the learner to separate UV-A, UV-B, and UV-C exposure, evaluate cannabis claims against dose-defined evidence, and prioritize worker controls.
- Required labels: UV-A, UV-B, UV-C, Dose
- Accuracy requirements: Ultraviolet bands differ in photon energy, plant response, material effects, and human hazard. A statement that a crop received 'UV' is scientifically inadequate without wavelength distribution, irradiance or photon flux, duration, distance, orientation, timing, and cumulative dose. | Cannabis claims that UV reliably increases cannabinoids are not supported as a universal rule. In a controlled study of one high-THC cultivar, the evaluated UV-A and UV-A plus UV-B treatments did not increase yield or cannabinoid content. A result from one cultivar and dose does not prove that all UV treatments are ineffective, but it directly rejects certainty. | UV can injure eyes and skin and degrade plastics, cables, sensors, and biological tissues. NIOSH-supported work in cannabis facilities recommends choosing low-UV-emitting lamps and using engineering controls, including interlocks for germicidal lamps. Access control, shielding, signage, training, exposure assessment, and verified shutdown states take priority over speculative crop benefit.
- Misconception guards: UV always boosts potency: Controlled cannabis evidence is mixed and treatment-specific; universal claims are unsupported. | UV-A is harmless: Risk depends on wavelength and dose; eye, skin, and material exposure still require assessment.

## 19. THC-ENC-121 — Essential Elements and the Criteria for Essentiality

- Family: `mechanism-process-diagram`
- Priority: **24**
- Purpose: Teach the learner to classify plant-essential elements by source and quantity while distinguishing essentiality from usefulness, abundance, and fertilizer marketing.
- Required labels: Essential element, Macronutrient, Micronutrient, Beneficial element
- Accuracy requirements: Plant essentiality is a biological claim, not a statement about whether an element appears in a fertilizer bottle. The classic criteria require that the plant cannot complete normal growth and reproduction without the element, that the function is specific rather than fully replaceable, and that the element acts directly in plant structure or metabolism. Carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen are acquired mainly from carbon dioxide and water; the remaining mineral elements are acquired chiefly as ions through roots. | Current general plant-nutrition teaching recognizes seventeen broadly essential elements: C, H, O, N, P, K, Ca, Mg, S, Fe, Mn, Zn, Cu, B, Mo, Cl, and Ni. Mineral management therefore begins with element identity, uptake form, source-water and substrate contributions, crop demand, and verified plant response.
- Misconception guards: Micronutrients barely matter: Quantity is small, but their enzyme, electron-transfer, reproductive, and structural functions are essential. | More complete means more ingredients: Completeness means adequate essential elements in available forms, not the longest additive list.

## 20. THC-ENC-122 — Nitrogen Forms, Uptake, and Metabolism

- Family: `diagnostic-decision-tree`
- Priority: **24**
- Purpose: Teach the learner to trace nitrate and ammonium from solution through uptake and assimilation while controlling total nitrogen, form, pH effect, and crop response separately.
- Required labels: Nitrate, Ammonium, Assimilation, Nitrogen-use efficiency
- Accuracy requirements: Nitrogen is incorporated into amino acids, proteins, nucleic acids, chlorophyll-associated machinery, cofactors, and many metabolites. ## 22. THC-ENC-124 — Potassium and Osmotic Regulation

- Family: `diagnostic-decision-tree`
- Priority: **24**

- Accuracy requirements: Potassium is not incorporated into a stable structural molecule in the way N enters protein or Mg enters chlorophyll.
