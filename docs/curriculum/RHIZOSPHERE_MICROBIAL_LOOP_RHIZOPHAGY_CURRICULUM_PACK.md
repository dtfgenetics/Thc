## Learning pathway
Audience: beginner through advanced. Prerequisites: ions, cell membranes, nitrogen, basic root anatomy. Estimated written learning time 55–75 minutes, excluding videos.
Learning outcomes: (1) label bulk soil, rhizosphere, rhizoplane and endosphere; (2) contrast root exudation, microbial decomposition, microbivory and proposed rhizophagy; (3) track N among organic, microbial, ammonium and nitrate pools; (4) distinguish observations from proposed mechanisms and causal proof; (5) design an experiment testing direct microbial nutrient transfer.

### Lesson A — Root-zone geography and exudation
Objective: identify where root–microbe interactions occur and why carbon exudation influences nutrient dynamics.
Key terms: rhizosphere, rhizoplane, endosphere, bulk soil, exudation, mucilage, biofilm, carbon allocation.
Core science: Photosynthetically fixed carbon travels to roots, where sugars, amino acids, organic acids, mucilage and specialized metabolites may enter the surrounding environment. Microorganisms respond to available carbon, nutrients, pH, oxygen and moisture. Exudates can support beneficial, neutral and harmful organisms. The rhizosphere has no universal fixed thickness; influence varies by root region, substance, soil and measurement method. An endophyte is internal; it is not automatically mutualistic.
Cannabis relevance: Cannabis roots host microbial communities, but generalized root exudate inventories cannot establish cultivar-specific compounds or rates. Avoid claiming that cannabis deliberately recruits only helpful species.
Visual specification: accurate cutaway identifying four root zones; separate gradient for dissolved carbon and microbes; accessible alt text explaining root influence.
Knowledge check: What distinguishes rhizoplane from endosphere? Why might a carbon exudate favor a potential pathogen as well as a beneficial bacterium?
Assessment rationale: surface-attached versus inside-tissue; exudate carbon is a resource, not a selective guarantee.

### Lesson B — Soil food web: decomposers and microbial predators
Objective: explain phagocytosis, bacterial grazing, predator assimilation, and nutrient excretion.
Key terms: microbivory, protist, amoeba, flagellate, bacterivorous nematode, phagosome, digestion, excretion.
Core science: Certain soil amoebae engulf bacteria using pseudopodia and phagosomes; other protists and nematodes ingest bacteria by different mechanisms. Digested prey provides carbon and elements to the predator. Nutrients not retained can return to surrounding pools, including ammonium. Predation changes bacterial community composition and can influence plant roots indirectly. Soil predators require suitable microhabitats; their presence does not prove increased crop yield.
Clarification: A macrophage is an animal immune cell, not a plant root cell or soil amoeba. Phagocytosis in amoebae must not be illustrated as if it were the same as the proposed root rhizophagy cycle.
Visuals: sequential amoeba phagocytosis illustration; predator–prey food-web network; diagram separating prey nitrogen assimilation and nitrogen excretion.
Knowledge check: Does the predator release all the nitrogen in a bacterium? How can predation change the bacterial community without increasing mineral N?
Assessment rationale: some N is retained in predator biomass; selective grazing and feedback effects are context-dependent.

### Lesson C — Nitrogen transformations and measurement
Objective: track N between organic residues, microbial biomass and inorganic forms without conflating transformations.
Key terms: ammonification, mineralization, immobilization, nitrification, nitrite, nitrate, denitrification, leaching, C:N.
Core science: Decomposition releases organic compounds; net mineralization adds inorganic ammonium when organic N is transformed; immobilization incorporates inorganic N into biomass. Nitrification oxidizes ammonium to nitrite and nitrate under usually aerobic conditions and can contribute acidity. Nitrate is commonly mobile and subject to leaching. Denitrification in reduced-oxygen microsites can convert nitrate/nitrogen oxides into gaseous products. Both mineralization and immobilization occur concurrently.
Interpretation: residue C:N ratios are rough indicators of net behavior, not universal thresholds. Total soil N cannot be substituted for extractable NH4+ and NO3- or for an incubation estimate of potentially mineralizable N. Symptoms of yellowing alone cannot diagnose immobilization.
Cannabis relevance: roots primarily acquire inorganic nitrogen through membrane transport systems. The actual N source and flux depend on growing medium, solution concentration, oxygen, root development and other factors.
Visuals: mass-balance network with bidirectional organic/inorganic arrows and explicit separate nitrification path; conceptual timeline marked NOT measured crop data; sampling protocol graphic.
Knowledge check: A soil test shows high total N but low nitrate: name at least three possible explanations and a distinguishing measurement.
Rationale: immobilization, slow mineralization, leaching, gaseous loss or uptake; measure ammonium, nitrate, substrate history, moisture and oxygen.

### Lesson D — Plant cell anatomy, ROS and the rhizophagy hypothesis
Objective: describe the model accurately without converting a hypothesis into a universal fact.
Key terms: cell wall, plasma membrane, apoplast, root epidermis, endophyte, reactive oxygen species, NADPH oxidase, rhizophagy.
Core science: Some microorganisms colonize interior plant tissues. White et al. (2018) proposed a repeating cycle in selected plants: association and entry into young root tissues, oxidative stress, potential extraction of microbial nutrients, and possible release of survivors near root hairs. Plant ROS participate in defense, signaling and development. Microbial localization in root tissue or ROS detection alone does not quantify host nutrient transfer.
Critical uncertainty: The complete cycle's frequency and nutritional contribution in Cannabis sativa have not been demonstrated. Distinguish observed structures, hypothesized transitions and confirmed transport. Do not claim cannabis has macrophages or that root cells use animal-style phagocytosis.
Visuals: cellular root wall/plasma membrane anatomy; proposed cycle with dashed arrows and conspicuous 'HYPOTHESIS' label; adjacent established ion-uptake pathway in solid arrows.
Knowledge check: What does microscopy of a bacterium inside a root establish? What additional experiment is needed to estimate N transfer?
Rationale: localization alone, not amount of nutrient uptake; isotope-label experiment with external mineralization controls.

### Lesson E — How to test competing explanations
Objective: design evidence distinguishing external mineralization, growth promotion and direct nutrient transfer.
Hypotheses: H1 microbes release mineral nitrogen outside roots; H2 microbes alter root growth or nutrient transport; H3 internalized microbes supply nutrients directly.
Proposed study: compare microbe-reduced controls, defined bacterial inoculation, defined bacteria plus grazer treatments, and stable isotope-labeled microbial biomass. Measure labeled dissolved N pools outside roots over time, root/shoot isotope enrichment, root architecture, microbial viability and localization, and total nitrogen accumulation. Use compatible sterile/nonsterile controls and appropriate replication. A positive label in the shoot alone does not distinguish H1 from H3. Researchers must also quantify nitrogen flux rates and account for labeling contamination and microbial leakage.
Scientific ethics: avoid unvalidated inference and environmental-release claims. No promise of benefit from a commercial microbe treatment.
Assessment: Explain why a 15N-labeled plant is not itself proof of rhizophagy, then specify two controls and a localization method.
Rationale: labeled material may mineralize externally and enter via ordinary transport; external solution sampling and endophyte localization provide complementary evidence.
