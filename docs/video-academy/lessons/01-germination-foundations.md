# THC Video Academy — Lesson 01: Germination Foundations

**Status:** Educational curriculum master; recording and publication NOT APPROVED  
**Audience:** Adult beginner/intermediate plant-science learners  
**Formats:** 7–10 minute human-narrated lesson; one 45–60 second Short; one six-frame image carousel; one Discord discussion  
**Narration:** Human-recorded only. No AI/TTS voice.  
**Media:** Documentary camera footage or clearly labeled scientific illustrations, each with creator, license and SHA-256 provenance.

## Learning outcomes

By the end of the lesson a learner can: (1) distinguish seed viability and dormancy from germination; (2) explain imbibition, respiration and radicle emergence; (3) identify the radicle, hypocotyl/epicotyl as applicable, cotyledons and early true leaves; (4) distinguish germination from emergence and establishment; (5) keep a consistent observation journal; and (6) state what an image can and cannot prove.

## Opening / scientific promise (0:00–0:35)

**Presenter script:** “A seed may look inactive, but inside is a living embryo. Germination is the biological process that lets that embryo resume growth. In this lesson we will see the progression from a dry seed to the first root and young seedling, and learn what matters when observing the process.”

**Visual:** Actual seed macro, slow physical camera slide. Detail inset is labeled **scientific diagram — not microscopy**. Avoid mature flowering plants.

## Chapter 1 — Anatomy and seed readiness (0:35–1:40)

A mature seed generally includes an embryo, seed coat, and nutrient reserves stored in endosperm and/or cotyledons, depending on the plant species. The seed coat protects the seed. The embryo contains the early axis that will form a root and shoot.

Do not equate seed size, color or an appealing photograph with guaranteed viability. A nonviable seed cannot germinate even under otherwise suitable conditions. Some viable seeds are dormant and require distinct environmental signals before they can germinate. Species differ.

**Shot sequence:** (a) 6-second physical macro pan; (b) labeled anatomical cross-section; (c) comparison of authentic dry seed close-ups with specimen identifiers.  
**On-screen glossary:** embryo, testa/seed coat, reserve, viability, dormancy.

## Chapter 2 — Water uptake (1:40–2:45)

The early stage called **imbibition** is the uptake of water by seed tissues. Hydration initiates physical and metabolic changes. Water is necessary, but it does not guarantee germination; seed viability, sufficient oxygen, and suitable environmental conditions are also relevant.

**Shot:** documented seed before and during water uptake. Use controlled diagrams for processes too small to see directly; do not simulate microscopic “water tunnels” as documentary evidence.  
**Misconception:** “A seed that swells has definitely germinated.” Correction: swelling is not the same as radicle emergence.

## Chapter 3 — Respiration, temperature and environment (2:45–4:05)

The embryo requires energy to resume growth. Cellular respiration depends on oxygen. Both too little moisture and conditions that severely restrict oxygen can disrupt germination. Appropriate temperature ranges and light or darkness requirements vary by species. Give no universal germination temperature or calendar that pretends all seeds respond identically.

**Shots:** scientifically labeled macro of substrate texture and aeration, original temperature-monitoring footage, animation distinguishing oxygen diffusion from visible gas bubbles.  
**On-screen caution:** Conditions and timing are species-dependent.

## Chapter 4 — Radicle emergence (4:05–5:15)

In many flowering plants, completion of germination is defined by **radicle protrusion** through the seed coat. The radicle is the embryonic root. This is a meaningful observable event—not merely a color change or swelling.

**Footage requirement:** true macro time-lapse or documented chronological photos showing a seed coat and an emerging radicle. If unavailable, use a labeled illustrative animation and explicitly say it is schematic. Do not substitute a seedling with mature leaves.

## Chapter 5 — Seedling emergence and early structure (5:15–6:45)

After radicle protrusion, the root system develops and the shoot emerges. Cotyledons (seed leaves) are not identical to the first true leaves. Their shapes and appearance depend on species. Seedling emergence above a medium and successful establishment are later stages; they should not be presented as interchangeable with completion of germination.

**Footage requirement:** genuine seedling macro with cotyledons and first true leaves labeled accurately, then full-plant establishing view.  
**Science callout:** “First root” and “first true leaf” are different developmental milestones.

## Chapter 6 — Observation and interpretation (6:45–8:00)

Record a date or elapsed-time marker, the specimen identifier, visible stage, and relevant measured environment. Take photographs from consistent angles. Report “radicle observed” when it is visible rather than inferring hidden processes from a still image. If an observation contradicts expectations, note the uncertainty instead of inventing a diagnosis.

**Demonstration:** real observation log on screen.  
**Example record:** “Day 2 — seed coat open; short pale radicle visible in macro image. No claim about later seedling health.”

## Assessment / training prompts

1. **What structure typically becomes visible first?** The radicle, or embryonic root.  
2. **Does a swollen seed always mean germination completed?** No; completion is commonly marked by radicle protrusion.  
3. **Why does oxygen matter?** Respiration supports metabolic activity and growth.  
4. **Is a universal germination duration scientifically justified?** No; variation depends on species, viability, dormancy and environment.  
5. **What distinguishes cotyledons from true leaves?** Cotyledons are embryonic seed leaves; true leaves develop subsequently.  
6. **What would a reliable observation record include?** Specimen, elapsed time/date, directly observed stage, relevant measured conditions and image evidence.
