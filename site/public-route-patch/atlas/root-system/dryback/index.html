Dryback

Dryback is the controlled drying cycle between irrigations. Managing it correctly balances oxygen delivery to roots, calcium transport to leaves, and the plant's water availability — all at once.

← Root OxygenModule IndexRoot Rot →
Core rule

Dryback delivers oxygen to roots and drives calcium transport to leaves simultaneously.

As substrate dries, air re-enters the pore space that was previously water-filled. This is the primary mechanism for root zone aeration in substrate grows. Simultaneously, moderate dryback maintains the mild water deficit that keeps stomata open and transpiration — and calcium delivery — active.

Measurement

Container weight is the most reliable dryback indicator.

Weigh the container at field capacity (fully watered and drained) and at a dry reference point. The percentage of weight lost between irrigations measures dryback directly. Visual inspection and finger-testing are unreliable — they assess the surface, not the root zone.

Dryback in practice

Establishing the rhythm

How to measure and manage dryback.

  1. Weigh your container fully watered and drained — this is field capacity (100%)
  2. Weigh it dry (empty + substrate dry weight) — this sets the 0% reference
  3. Calculate target weight for your desired dryback percentage
  4. Irrigate when the container reaches the target dry weight, not on a schedule
  5. Adjust irrigation volume to return to field capacity in a single event (or split shots)
  6. Track and record — dryback rhythm tells you if plant demand is increasing (flower) or decreasing (stress)
Dryback problems

What happens when it is wrong.

  • Too little dryback: anaerobic root zone; pythium risk; calcium deficiency from low transpiration; slow growth
  • Too much dryback: wilting; stomatal closure; transpiration stops; calcium and mineral delivery halts; acute root hair death in severe cases
  • Inconsistent dryback: plant cannot establish transpiration rhythm; VPD response becomes erratic; EC fluctuations in coco from incomplete nutrient distribution
  • Scheduling by the clock: plant demand varies with VPD, light cycle, and stage — weight-based irrigation is more accurate than timed schedules
