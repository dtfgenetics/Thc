Water Uptake

Water uptake through roots depends on osmotic gradients, root pressure, and the transpiration stream. Understanding the mechanics clarifies when irrigation decisions and substrate moisture affect plant health.

← RhizosphereModule IndexNutrient Uptake →
Core rule

Water moves into roots by osmosis — from lower to higher solute concentration.

Root cells maintain higher solute concentration than the surrounding soil water. This creates the osmotic gradient that drives passive water entry. When substrate EC is very high, this gradient reverses — water moves out of roots into the substrate, causing osmotic stress.

Three pathways

Water can move through, around, or between root cells.

The apoplastic pathway (through cell walls), symplastic pathway (through cytoplasm and plasmodesmata), and transmembrane pathway (through aquaporin channels) each contribute to uptake. Aquaporins are the regulated channel — their expression responds to drought, temperature, and nutrient status.

Water uptake mechanics

Osmosis at root hair

Water in substrate moves into root hair cells along the osmotic gradient — lower solute concentration outside to higher inside.

Cortex movement

Water moves through the root cortex via apoplastic, symplastic, or transmembrane pathways toward the central vascular cylinder.

Casparian strip

A waterproof band of suberin in the endodermis forces all water through the cell membrane — the plant's last point of selective entry control before the vascular system.

Xylem loading

Water enters root xylem vessels. Mineral ions are actively transported in alongside. This is where root pressure (positive push) originates.

Transpiration pull

Evaporation at leaf stomata creates negative pressure that pulls the water column up through xylem continuously. The dominant driving force above seedling stage.

Leaf delivery

Water arrives at leaf vascular bundles and distributes through mesophyll cells before exiting through stomata, depositing minerals along the way.
