Root Rot

Root rot is not a single disease — it is a cluster of water mould and fungal infections that exploit oxygen-depleted, warm, or stressed root zones. Prevention and early identification are far more effective than treatment.

← DrybackModule IndexRoot Zone Diagnostics →
Core rule

Root rot requires three conditions: pathogen presence, stressed roots, and moisture.

Pythium and Fusarium are ubiquitous — they exist in almost every growing environment. They become problems when root stress (oxygen deprivation, temperature stress, or physical damage) and sustained moisture combine to allow pathogen establishment and spread. Remove any one condition and progression stops.

Prevention over treatment

Once established, root rot is very difficult to fully reverse.

Root hairs destroyed by pythium do not regenerate quickly. A plant that has lost 40%+ of its root mass to rot will struggle to recover even if the pathogen is eliminated. Prevention through correct oxygen levels, temperature, and drainage is far more effective than any treatment protocol.

Primary root rot pathogens

water mould

Pythium spp.

Produces zoospores that swim through saturated substrate toward root exudates. The most common cause of root rot in cannabis grows. Thrives at 20–26°C with high moisture and low oxygen. Distinctive slimy, brown root tissue with fishy smell.

water mould

Phytophthora spp.

Closely related to Pythium; similar infection mechanism. Less common in indoor production but highly destructive. Causes crown rot at the stem base as well as root rot. Brown lesions at soil line with water-soaked appearance.

fungal

Fusarium oxysporum

Soil-borne fungal pathogen. Unlike Pythium, Fusarium can colonise the vascular system — causing vascular wilt that presents as sudden collapse even with healthy-appearing roots. Brown vascular discolouration in stem cross-section is diagnostic.

fungal

Rhizoctonia solani

Most destructive at seedling stage — causes damping off (stem collapse at soil line). Also causes root rot in older plants under stress. Brown, shrunken lesions at the crown and on lateral roots.

Identification and response

Identification

How to confirm root rot.

  1. Carefully remove plant from container at lights-off — root inspection without light stress
  2. Observe root colour: brown, grey, or black vs white/cream healthy
  3. Squeeze a root between fingers: healthy roots are firm; infected roots are slimy and break easily
  4. Smell the root mass: fishy, rotten, or sour odour indicates active anaerobic pathogen
  5. Check the stem base for brown lesions or water-soaked tissue — indicates Phytophthora or Rhizoctonia
Response protocol

Once root rot is confirmed.

  1. Improve drainage and reduce irrigation frequency immediately — remove the moisture condition
  2. Improve aeration — raise dissolved oxygen in hydro; allow substrate dryback in coco/soil
  3. Reduce root zone temperature below 22°C — slows pathogen reproduction
  4. Remove and dispose of heavily infected plants to prevent spread
  5. Consider beneficial inoculants (Trichoderma, Bacillus subtilis) to re-colonise after environment correction
  6. Avoid hydrogen peroxide — it sterilises all biology; beneficial microbes that compete with pythium are lost
