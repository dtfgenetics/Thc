Leaf Anatomy

The cannabis leaf is a factory, gas exchanger, diagnostic surface, and transport hub. Seven distinct tissue layers each with a different role — understanding the structure explains how visible symptoms are generated.

Module IndexPhotosynthesis →
Core structure

Seven tissue layers, each with a distinct function.

From the waxy cuticle outside to the vascular bundles running through the centre, every layer is optimised for a specific role. Symptoms appear at the surface but originate deeper.

Diagnostic principle

Surface symptoms reflect events inside each layer.

Chloroplast dysfunction shows as yellowing. Vascular blockage as wilting or marginal scorch. Cell membrane damage as necrosis. Reading symptoms requires knowing which structure produced them.

Leaf layers

surface

Cuticle

Waxy lipid layer on the upper epidermis. Prevents water loss and provides the first pathogen barrier. Reduces foliar spray uptake — consider this when applying contact products.

surface

Upper Epidermis

Single layer of transparent, tightly packed cells with no chloroplasts. Allows light penetration. Contains cystolithic hair bases — not secretory trichomes.

photosynthesis

Palisade Mesophyll

Densely packed columnar cells loaded with chloroplasts, just below the upper epidermis. The primary photosynthesis site. Chloroplast density here sets the leaf's light-capture capacity.

photosynthesis

Spongy Mesophyll

Loosely arranged cells with large air spaces for CO₂ diffusion. Contains chloroplasts at lower density than palisade. Connects substomatal chambers to palisade cells.

transport

Vascular Bundles

Xylem and phloem through every vein. Xylem delivers water and minerals up from roots; phloem moves sugars and mobile nutrients out. Visible as raised veins on leaf undersides.

gas exchange

Lower Epidermis & Stomata

100–300 stomata per mm² on the lower surface. Guard cells control each pore in response to light, CO₂, water status, and abscisic acid.

Anatomy-to-symptom map

Where symptoms originate

Layer determines symptom type.

  • Interveinal yellowing: chloroplast dysfunction in palisade/spongy cells — nutrient, pH, or light cause
  • Margin scorch: salt/toxin accumulation where xylem terminates at leaf edges
  • Tip burn: calcium delivery failure to outermost cells — VPD or transpiration issue
  • Brown spots with halos: localised cell death — pathogen or chemical contact
  • Wilting: xylem supply failure — root damage, stem blockage, or severe water deficit
Field observation

Using anatomy to interpret symptoms.

  1. Check if leaf veins are preserved or affected — vein preservation with interveinal yellowing is a key distinction
  2. Check the leaf underside: silvering, webbing, and stippling indicate stomatal or lower-epidermis damage
  3. Note where in the canopy symptoms appear — determines whether mobile or immobile nutrient is involved
  4. Describe the boundary between affected and healthy tissue — sharp edges suggest contact damage; gradients suggest systemic causes
