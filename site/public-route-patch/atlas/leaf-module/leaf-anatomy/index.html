Leaf Anatomy

The cannabis leaf has seven distinct tissue layers, each with a specific role. Understanding the structure explains how visible symptoms are generated — chloroplast dysfunction, vascular failure, cell membrane damage — all present differently at the surface.

Module IndexModule IndexPhotosynthesis →
Structure

Seven tissue layers — each produces different symptoms.

Cuticle → upper epidermis → palisade mesophyll → spongy mesophyll → vascular bundle → lower epidermis → stomata. Symptoms at the leaf surface trace back to which layer was disrupted.

Diagnostic principle

The symptom location inside the leaf tells you what caused it.

Interveinal yellowing = palisade/spongy chloroplast dysfunction. Marginal scorch = xylem terminus accumulation. Tip burn = calcium delivery failure. Wilting = xylem supply failure.

Layer by layer

surface

Cuticle

Waxy lipid layer on the upper epidermis. Prevents water loss and provides the first barrier to pathogens and contact pesticides. A thick, waxy cuticle reduces foliar spray uptake.

surface

Upper Epidermis

Single layer of transparent, tightly packed cells with no chloroplasts. Allows light penetration. Contains cystolithic hair bases — not secretory trichomes.

photosynthesis

Palisade Mesophyll

Densely packed columnar cells loaded with chloroplasts just below the upper epidermis. The primary photosynthesis site. Chloroplast density here sets the leaf's light-capture capacity.

photosynthesis

Spongy Mesophyll

Loosely arranged cells with large air spaces for CO₂ diffusion. Contains chloroplasts at lower density. Connects substomatal chambers to palisade cells.

transport

Vascular Bundles

Xylem and phloem through every vein. Xylem delivers water and minerals up from roots; phloem moves sugars and mobile nutrients out. Visible as raised veins on leaf undersides.

gas exchange

Lower Epidermis & Stomata

100–300 stomata per mm² on the lower surface. Guard cells control each pore in response to light, CO₂, water status, and abscisic acid.

Anatomy-to-symptom map

Symptom origins

Layer determines symptom type.

  • Interveinal yellowing: chloroplast dysfunction in palisade/spongy cells
  • Margin scorch: salt/toxin accumulation where xylem terminates at leaf edges
  • Tip burn: calcium delivery failure to outermost cells — VPD or transpiration issue
  • Brown spots with halos: localised cell death — pathogen or chemical contact
  • Wilting: xylem supply failure — root damage, stem blockage, or water deficit
Field observation

Using anatomy to interpret symptoms.

  1. Check if leaf veins are preserved or affected — vein preservation with interveinal yellowing is a key distinction
  2. Check the leaf underside: silvering, webbing, and stippling indicate stomatal or lower-epidermis damage
  3. Note canopy position — determines whether mobile or immobile nutrient is involved
  4. Describe the boundary: sharp edges suggest contact damage; gradients suggest systemic causes
