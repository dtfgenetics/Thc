Necrosis

Necrosis is irreversible cell death producing brown, black, or tan tissue. Like chlorosis, the pattern and location are the diagnostic signal — not the colour of dead tissue alone.

← ChlorosisModule IndexLeaf Curl →
Core rule

Necrosis indicates cells are dead — the question is why and where.

Brown or black tissue with no recovery potential means cells have reached the point of irreversible damage. This can be nutritional, environmental, pathogenic, or physical. The pattern narrows the field significantly.

Key distinction

Edge of necrosis tells you more than the dead tissue itself.

A sharp yellow halo around dead tissue suggests pathogen involvement. A gradual fade from yellow to brown suggests nutritional or environmental cause. An abrupt change with no halo suggests contact damage or extreme heat.

Necrosis patterns

PatternLocationMost likely causes
Margin / edge necrosisOld leaves, tips and edgesPotassium deficiency, salt accumulation (tip burn), excess heat at leaf edge
Tip necrosis, new growthNewest leaves, growing tipCalcium deficiency from low VPD or root damage, boron deficiency
Brown spots with yellow haloAny positionFungal or bacterial pathogen (Botrytis, septoria, bacterial leaf spot)
Tan/brown spots without haloUpper canopyLight burn, heat stress, contact pesticide damage
Large irregular brown areasMid-canopy, spreadingPythium or Fusarium root damage — systemic collapse of vascular supply
Small dark pin-point spotsScattered, upper canopyThrip feeding damage, russet mite scarring, late-stage spider mite

Observation and response

Necrosis assessment

What to examine before acting.

  1. Is it spreading actively or stable? Spreading necrosis with a yellow border suggests a live pathogen — stable necrosis may be residual from a past event
  2. Are other plants affected? Uniform spread through the room suggests environmental or nutrient cause; isolated plants suggest pest or pathogen
  3. What is the distribution within the plant? Old leaves, new leaves, or random position narrows the cause significantly
  4. Remove and examine a representative leaf with a hand lens — check for fungal sporulation, pest evidence, or pure chemical burn appearance
Do not

Common necrosis mistakes.

  • Removing affected leaves without diagnosing — removes evidence and may spread a pathogen
  • Treating as potassium deficiency without checking pH and root condition — marginal necrosis has multiple causes
  • Spraying fungicides onto tissue that is physically burned or salt-damaged — fungicide-induced phytotoxicity adds another layer of damage
  • Concluding calcium deficiency from tip necrosis without checking VPD — root-zone calcium may be correct
