Transpiration

Transpiration is the movement of water from roots through the plant to the atmosphere through stomata. It drives calcium transport, mineral delivery, and is the mechanism through which VPD affects plant physiology.

← StomataModule IndexChlorosis →
Core rule

Transpiration is the engine that delivers calcium to leaves.

Water moving through xylem carries dissolved calcium, magnesium, and other xylem-mobile minerals. Without adequate transpiration, these minerals accumulate below and fail to reach new growth — causing deficiency symptoms even when root-zone supply is correct.

VPD drives it

VPD determines transpiration rate — not humidity alone.

Vapour pressure deficit is the difference between air's water-holding capacity and actual water vapour content. The higher the VPD, the greater the evaporative pull through open stomata, the faster water moves from leaf to atmosphere.

The transpiration pathway — 6 steps

Root uptake

Water enters root cells by osmosis. Mineral ions are actively transported into root xylem vessels alongside water.

Xylem ascent

Water moves upward through xylem as a continuous column, pulled by evaporation at the leaf — cohesion-tension mechanism.

Leaf delivery

Water reaches leaf vascular bundles, then moves through mesophyll cell walls toward substomatal air spaces.

Evaporation

Water evaporates from wet cell surfaces into substomatal cavities, becoming water vapour.

Stomatal exit

Water vapour diffuses out through open stomata along the vapour pressure gradient — faster when VPD is higher.

Mineral deposit

Calcium and xylem-mobile minerals are deposited in leaf cells as water evaporates. Rate directly proportional to transpiration rate.

Too low vs too high

Low transpiration (low VPD)

Consequences.

  • Calcium deficiency on new growth: insufficient delivery regardless of root-zone supply
  • Tip burn and leaf distortion: most common in high-humidity, low-VPD conditions
  • Botrytis risk: low transpiration = high surface humidity = pathogen conditions
  • Slower growth rate: reduced mineral supply slows cell expansion and division
  • Extended drying cycle: plants using less water — root oxygen stress risk increases
High transpiration (high VPD)

Consequences.

  • Wilting: water loss exceeds root uptake capacity — stomata close, growth halts
  • Marginal salt scorch: minerals concentrate where water exits at leaf edges
  • Increased irrigation demand: faster drying cycles require more frequent watering
  • Root zone acidification: higher nutrient uptake rate alters pH more rapidly
  • Stress response: ABA released, slowing growth even when all other inputs are adequate
