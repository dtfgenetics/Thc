Transpiration

Transpiration moves water from roots through the plant to the atmosphere. Evaporative water loss helps drive xylem flow, interacts with stomatal conductance and water supply, and contributes to mineral transport.

← StomataModule IndexModule IndexChlorosis →
Core rule

Xylem water flow contributes to calcium movement.

Xylem flow transports dissolved minerals, including calcium, but delivery also depends on roots, tissue development and environmental conditions. Low flow alone does not prove a deficiency.

VPD drives it

VPD influences evaporative demand alongside stomatal conductance.

VPD is the difference between saturated and actual vapour pressure at a given temperature. Higher VPD can increase the potential for evaporation, but plants may regulate stomatal opening and water flow.

The transpiration pathway

Root uptake

Water enters through root tissues along water-potential gradients; ions move through regulated transport pathways.

Xylem ascent

Water moves upward as a continuous column, pulled by evaporation at the leaf — cohesion-tension mechanism.

Leaf delivery

Water reaches leaf vascular bundles, then moves through mesophyll cell walls toward substomatal air spaces.

Evaporation

Water evaporates from wet cell surfaces into substomatal cavities, becoming water vapour.

Stomatal exit

Water vapour diffuses through stomata along a vapour gradient; actual flux also depends on stomatal opening and boundary-layer resistance.

Mineral deposit

Mineral distribution depends on xylem flow, tissue development, plant physiology and mineral availability; it is not simply proportional to evaporation.

Too low vs too high

Low VPD → low transpiration

Consequences.

  • Mineral distribution changes — possible altered transport; confirm the cause before treating
  • Tip damage or distortion — nonspecific observations that require root-zone and pest checks
  • Moisture-associated pathogen risk — depends on wetness duration, pathogen presence and other conditions
  • Extended drying cycle — plants using less water; root oxygen stress risk increases
High VPD → excess transpiration

Consequences.

  • Wilting — can occur when water supply is insufficient relative to demand; confirm root and water status
  • Marginal injury — may reflect several stresses; inspect salinity and environment
  • Stress response — stomatal and hormonal regulation may affect water use and carbon uptake
  • Changing irrigation demand — use measured media moisture and plant water use rather than fixed schedules
