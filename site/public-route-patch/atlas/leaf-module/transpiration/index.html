Transpiration

Transpiration moves water from roots through the plant to the atmosphere. It drives calcium transport, mineral delivery, and is the mechanism through which VPD affects the entire plant.

← StomataModule IndexModule IndexChlorosis →
Core rule

Transpiration is the engine that delivers calcium to leaves.

Water moving through xylem carries dissolved calcium and other xylem-mobile minerals. Without adequate transpiration, calcium fails to reach new growth — producing deficiency symptoms even when root-zone supply is correct.

VPD drives it

VPD determines transpiration rate — not humidity alone.

Vapour pressure deficit is the difference between air's water-holding capacity and actual water vapour content. The higher the VPD, the greater the evaporative pull through open stomata.

The transpiration pathway

Root uptake

Water enters root cells by osmosis. Mineral ions are actively transported into root xylem vessels alongside water.

Xylem ascent

Water moves upward as a continuous column, pulled by evaporation at the leaf — cohesion-tension mechanism.

Leaf delivery

Water reaches leaf vascular bundles, then moves through mesophyll cell walls toward substomatal air spaces.

Evaporation

Water evaporates from wet cell surfaces into substomatal cavities, becoming water vapour.

Stomatal exit

Water vapour diffuses out through open stomata along the vapour pressure gradient — faster when VPD is higher.

Mineral deposit

Calcium and xylem-mobile minerals are deposited in leaf cells as water evaporates — rate proportional to transpiration rate.

Too low vs too high

Low VPD → low transpiration

Consequences.

  • Calcium deficiency on new growth — insufficient delivery regardless of root-zone supply
  • Tip burn and leaf distortion — most common in high-humidity, low-VPD conditions
  • Botrytis risk — high surface humidity enables pathogen colonisation
  • Extended drying cycle — plants using less water; root oxygen stress risk increases
High VPD → excess transpiration

Consequences.

  • Wilting — water loss exceeds root uptake capacity; stomata close
  • Marginal salt scorch — minerals concentrate where water exits at leaf edges
  • Stress response — ABA released, slowing growth even when all other inputs are adequate
  • Increased irrigation demand — faster drying cycles require more frequent watering
