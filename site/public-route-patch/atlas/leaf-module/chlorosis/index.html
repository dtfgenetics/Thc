Chlorosis

Chlorosis is yellowing from reduced chlorophyll. It is a symptom category — not a diagnosis. Pattern and location are the primary diagnostic tools. The overall colour tells you almost nothing on its own.

← TranspirationModule IndexModule IndexNecrosis →
Core rule

Pattern and location guide the investigation — colour alone is not a diagnosis.

Uniform yellowing, interveinal yellowing on old leaves, and pale new growth with green veins are three completely different situations with different causes and different correct responses.

Mobile vs immobile

Old versus new tissue helps narrow the possibilities.

Nutrients differ in phloem mobility. Nitrogen, magnesium, potassium, and phosphorus deficiencies often become visible on older leaves first; calcium and iron availability problems often affect young growth. Sulfur, zinc, copper, and other elements can show variable or species-dependent mobility. Leaf position is a clue, not a diagnosis: confirm root health, pH, EC, environment, and symptom progression.

Observation sequence

Before acting

Correct sequence.

  1. Note where yellowing appears: old or new tissue, canopy position
  2. Describe pattern: uniform, interveinal, marginal, blotchy, progression direction
  3. Check if green veins are preserved
  4. Measure root-zone pH and EC
  5. Inspect roots and irrigation history
  6. Check VPD and recent environmental changes
  7. Build a cause list — test the most likely with the smallest intervention
Common errors

What to avoid.

  • Relying on photographs without checking root-zone conditions — nutrient availability depends on the growing medium
  • Adding nitrogen for any general yellowing without checking canopy position first
  • Not checking roots before treating foliage — root damage disrupts uptake of everything
  • Treating normal senescence as an emergency
  • Making multiple adjustments at once — impossible to know what worked
