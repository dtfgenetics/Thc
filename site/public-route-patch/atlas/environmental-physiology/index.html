Environmental Physiology

Light, temperature, humidity, VPD, CO₂, and airflow are not independent dials — they interact through plant physiology. Adjusting one without understanding how it affects the others is a reliable way to create new problems while trying to fix existing ones.

Core rule

Every environmental parameter affects plant physiology through the same organ — the leaf.

Stomata open and close in response to light, CO₂, humidity, and temperature simultaneously. Changes to any one factor shift the others' effects. VPD sets the transpiration demand that determines nutrient uptake speed; light intensity sets photosynthesis rate that determines CO₂ demand; temperature sets enzyme activity that determines metabolic rate. Adjust one, measure all.

First measurement principle

Measure before you adjust

Most environmental problems in controlled growing are created by adjusting without measuring — "it feels warm" rather than "temperature at canopy is 29°C with a VPD of 1.8 kPa." Record your baseline before making changes, and give the plant enough time to show its response before making a second adjustment.

Key parameters

What each environmental factor controls

light

Light — PPFD & DLI

Photosynthetically active radiation drives photosynthesis. PPFD (μmol/m²/s) is instantaneous intensity; DLI (mol/m²/day) is the total daily light dose. Both matter — a high-PPFD short photoperiod may deliver less DLI than a lower PPFD with a longer day.

temperature

Temperature & Enzyme Activity

Photosynthesis, respiration, and nutrient uptake are all enzyme-driven processes with temperature optima. Canopy temperature (not air temperature) is the correct measurement. Leaf-to-air temperature offset of −1 to −3°C is normal in a healthy transpiring plant.

humidity

Humidity & Vapour Pressure Deficit

Relative humidity does not drive plant transpiration — VPD does. VPD is the difference between the water vapour the air could hold at a given temperature and how much it actually holds. High VPD forces high transpiration; low VPD restricts it. Calcium transport is transpiration-dependent.

CO₂

CO₂ & Photosynthetic Ceiling

Atmospheric CO₂ (~420 ppm) sets the photosynthetic ceiling at typical light levels. Supplemental CO₂ (800–1500 ppm) raises that ceiling — but only if light intensity and temperature are high enough to drive additional photosynthesis. CO₂ supplementation without adequate PPFD and temperature produces almost no response.

airflow

Airflow & Boundary Layer

Gentle airflow removes the boundary layer of humid, CO₂-depleted air surrounding each leaf. It also provides mechanical stimulus (thigmomorphogenesis) that strengthens stems. Turbulent flow reduces boundary layer resistance; stagnant air creates microclimates that promote pathogen conditions.

root

Root Zone Temperature

Root enzyme activity, water uptake rate, and pathogen susceptibility are all temperature-dependent. Root zone temperature below 18°C slows uptake significantly; above 28°C promotes Pythium. Root zone temperature diverging from air temperature by more than ~8°C creates uptake stress even when foliar environment is correct.

Interaction effects to understand first

  • Temperature ↑ + RH constant → VPD ↑: raising temperature without increasing RH always raises VPD and transpiration demand
  • High VPD → calcium deficiency symptoms: transpiration drives calcium — low VPD = low calcium transport to new growth
  • Low CO₂ + high light: photosynthesis is CO₂-limited before light-limited at normal atmospheric levels above ~600 μmol/m²/s
  • Airflow + high humidity: adequate airflow prevents botrytis at humidity levels that would otherwise be risky
  • Root zone cold + foliar correct: plant shows nutrient and water stress symptoms despite correct above-ground environment

Environmental symptom map

  • Tip burn (new growth): high VPD or low airflow → calcium transport failure
  • Bleaching / light burn: PPFD above plant tolerance at current temperature and CO₂ level
  • Leaf curl up (taco): high temperature, high VPD, or heat stress
  • Leaf curl down: overwatering, root problems, or high humidity with low VPD
  • Stunted new growth: low temperature slowing cell division, or extreme VPD
  • Botrytis in dense buds: high humidity + inadequate airflow in canopy
  • Sudden mid-day wilt: high VPD spike, root zone temperature excess, or root damage
