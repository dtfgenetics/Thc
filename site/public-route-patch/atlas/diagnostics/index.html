Observation & Differential Diagnostics

A visible symptom is evidence — not a diagnosis. Structured observation separates what you can see from what it means, and protects against the most common diagnostic error: acting on one possible explanation before ruling out the others.

⌕ The core rule

Describe the evidence precisely first. Location, distribution pattern, affected tissue age, progression timeline, and measurable context. Only then build a list of explanations that fit — and test each one against what you cannot see as much as what you can.

Why this matters

Most plant-health errors are not wrong knowledge — they are pattern-matching too fast. A yellowing lower leaf can mean nitrogen mobilisation, overwatering, root damage, light exclusion, or normal senescence. Treating one without ruling out the others often makes the real problem worse.

Structured observation protocol

Work through these in sequence. Skipping early steps to reach a conclusion faster is how misdiagnosis happens.

Describe the visible pattern

Colour, texture, shape change, size. Use neutral language — "yellowing interveinal" not "magnesium deficiency." What you describe should match what a photograph would show.

Map the distribution

Old leaves only, new growth only, whole plant, one branch, one side of the canopy, spots vs uniform fading vs margin burn. Distribution is the single strongest diagnostic signal.

Establish the timeline

When did it first appear? Has it progressed? Fast or slow? Did it follow an environmental change, feed change, IPM treatment, transplant, or training event?

Check the root zone

pH, EC, moisture level, runoff pH, container weight, substrate condition. Most foliar symptoms originate below the soil line. Check roots before treating leaves.

Check the environment

Temperature, humidity, VPD, airflow, DLI, CO₂. Many symptoms attributed to nutrition are environmental — calcium deficiency symptoms under high VPD, for example.

Check for pests and pathogens

Hand lens inspection of leaf undersides, stem base, soil surface, and new growth. Rule out biotic causes before concluding abiotic.

Check mobility

Mobile nutrients (N, P, K, Mg, Mo) deficiency symptoms appear first on old tissue. Immobile nutrients (Ca, Fe, Mn, B, Cu, Zn) appear on new growth. Distribution does not lie.

Build a differential, not a verdict

List all explanations that fit the evidence. Rank by probability given your specific context. Test the most likely explanation first with the smallest intervention — not the most dramatic one.

Common misdiagnosis patterns

These are the most frequently misread situations. Each resolves differently from what the surface symptoms suggest.

Calcium deficiency vs. VPD stress

Tip burn and leaf curl look identical. Measure VPD before adding calcium. High VPD causes transpiration stress that blocks calcium uptake even when pH and supply are correct.

Iron deficiency vs. pH lockout

Interveinal yellowing on new growth almost always means pH above 7.0 in soil or above 6.5 in coco/hydro. Test pH first — adding iron at locked-out pH does nothing.

Nitrogen deficiency vs. normal senescence

Old leaves yellowing on a healthy plant in late flower is expected nitrogen mobilisation, not a deficiency. Check if it's progressing rapidly upward before acting.

Overwatering vs. underwatering

Both cause drooping. Overwatered leaves droop and feel soft. Underwatered leaves droop and feel papery. Container weight is faster and more reliable than visual inspection.

Root rot vs. heat stress

Both cause wilting at lights-on. Root rot wilting persists into the dark period. Heat stress wilting recovers when temperature drops. Check roots before treating foliage.

Broad mite vs. genetic mutation

New growth distortion, cupping, and abnormal development are classic broad mite signs. Rule out pests with a 60× loupe before concluding a plant is a phenotype anomaly.
