Differential
Diagnostics

A visible symptom is evidence — not a diagnosis. Structured observation separates what you can see from what it means, and protects against the single most common plant-health error: acting on one explanation before ruling out the others.

Core principle

Describe the evidence precisely before naming a cause.

Location, distribution pattern, affected tissue age, progression timeline, and measurable context first. Build a list of explanations that fit — then test each against what you cannot see as much as what you can.

⌕ The observation rule

A yellowing lower leaf can mean nitrogen mobilisation, overwatering, root damage, light exclusion, or normal senescence. The evidence is the same — the cause is not. Skipping early observation steps to reach a conclusion faster is how misdiagnosis happens.

Structured observation protocol

Work through these in sequence. Each step constrains the next.

Describe the pattern

Colour, texture, shape change, size. Use neutral language — "yellowing interveinal" not "magnesium deficiency." What you describe should match a photograph.

Map the distribution

Old leaves only, new growth only, whole plant, one branch, one side, spots vs uniform fading vs margin burn. Distribution is the strongest single diagnostic signal.

Establish the timeline

When did it first appear? Has it progressed? Fast or slow? Did it follow an environmental change, feed change, IPM treatment, transplant, or training event?

Check the root zone

pH, EC, moisture level, container weight, substrate condition. Most foliar symptoms originate below the soil line. Check roots before treating leaves.

Check the environment

Temperature, humidity, VPD, airflow, DLI, CO₂. Many symptoms attributed to nutrition are environmental — calcium deficiency under high VPD, for example.

Inspect for pests

Hand lens inspection of leaf undersides, stem base, soil surface, new growth. Rule out biotic causes before concluding abiotic.

Check mobility

Mobile nutrients (N, P, K, Mg, Mo) show deficiency on old tissue. Immobile (Ca, Fe, Mn, B, Cu, Zn) show on new growth. Distribution does not lie.

Build a differential

List all explanations that fit the evidence. Rank by probability. Test the most likely with the smallest intervention — not the most dramatic one.

Common misdiagnosis patterns

These are the most frequently misread situations. Each resolves differently from what the surface symptom suggests.

misdiagnosis

Calcium deficiency vs VPD stress

Tip burn and new-growth curl look identical. Measure VPD before adding calcium. High VPD causes transpiration stress that blocks calcium uptake even when pH and supply are correct.

misdiagnosis

Iron deficiency vs pH lockout

Interveinal yellowing on new growth almost always means pH above 7.0 in soil or above 6.5 in coco/hydro. Test pH first — adding iron at locked-out pH does nothing.

misdiagnosis

Nitrogen deficiency vs normal senescence

Old leaves yellowing on a healthy plant in late flower is expected nitrogen mobilisation. Check if it's progressing rapidly upward before acting.

misdiagnosis

Overwatering vs underwatering

Both cause drooping. Overwatered leaves droop soft. Underwatered leaves droop papery. Container weight is faster and more reliable than visual inspection.

misdiagnosis

Root rot vs heat stress

Both cause mid-day wilting. Root rot persists into the dark period. Heat stress recovers when temperature drops. Check roots before treating foliage.

misdiagnosis

Broad mite vs genetic mutation

New growth distortion and cupping are classic broad mite signs. Rule out pests with a 60× loupe before concluding phenotype anomaly.
