Stem & Vascular System

The stem is the transport highway between roots and canopy. Xylem moves water and minerals upward; phloem moves sugars and signals downward. Disrupting either pathway affects the whole plant — not just the site of damage.

Core rule

Transport disruption affects the whole plant — symptoms appear at a distance.

A damaged or compressed vascular bundle at a training bend can cause wilting, calcium deficiency symptoms, or growth stalling well above and below the damage point. Diagnosis must check the stem before concluding the symptom is nutritional or environmental.

Transport direction

Xylem vs phloem: opposite directions, different content

Xylem carries water and dissolved minerals from roots upward — it is passive, driven by transpiration pull. Phloem carries sugars and mobile nutrients bidirectionally from source leaves to growing sinks. Damage to one pathway has different consequences from the other.

Stem health indicators

What to look for on the stem

  • Healthy: firm, green to light purple, odour-free at the base, no soft spots
  • Pythium/root rot base: dark, soft, mushy tissue at soil line, fishy or rotten smell
  • Training damage: visible bruising, flattened vascular tissue, swelling above the bend point
  • Pest boring: frass, entry holes, sawdust-like debris on stem surface
  • Nitrogen excess: thick, lush, dark green stems — often paired with soft tissue that bruises easily
Internodal measurement guide

Using internode length as a diagnostic

  1. Measure between nodes 3 and 5 from the top as a representative vegetative stretch indicator
  2. Record the measurement at the same time each week — growth rate matters as much as length
  3. Compare to a healthy baseline for the genetics — some varieties have naturally longer internodes
  4. Sudden internode shortening mid-veg can indicate heat stress, light intensity increase, or nutrient toxicity
  5. Sudden internode stretching mid-flower usually indicates inadequate light or far-red penetration through the canopy
