Stem &
Vascular
System

The stem is the transport highway between roots and canopy. Xylem moves water and minerals upward; phloem moves sugars and signals downward. Disrupting either pathway affects the whole plant — symptoms appear at a distance from the damage site.

Core rule

Transport disruption causes symptoms at a distance from the damage site.

A damaged or compressed vascular bundle at a training bend can cause wilting, calcium deficiency symptoms, or growth stalling well above and below the damage point. Diagnosis must check the stem before concluding the symptom is nutritional or environmental.

Transport direction

Xylem and phloem carry different cargo in different directions.

Xylem carries water and dissolved minerals from roots upward — passive, driven by transpiration pull. Phloem carries sugars and mobile nutrients bidirectionally from source leaves to growing sinks. Damage to one has different consequences from the other.

Stem health & measurement

Stem health indicators

What to look for on the stem.

  • Healthy: firm, green to light purple, odour-free at the base, no soft spots
  • Pythium / root rot base: dark, soft, mushy tissue at soil line, fishy or rotten smell
  • Training damage: visible bruising, flattened vascular tissue, swelling above the bend
  • Pest boring: frass, entry holes, sawdust-like debris on stem surface
  • Nitrogen excess: thick, lush, dark green stems — often paired with soft tissue that bruises easily
Measurement guide

Using internode length diagnostically.

  1. Measure between nodes 3 and 5 from the top as the representative vegetative stretch indicator
  2. Record at the same time each week — growth rate matters as much as length
  3. Compare to a healthy baseline for the genetics — some varieties have naturally longer internodes
  4. Sudden shortening mid-veg can indicate heat stress, light intensity increase, or nutrient toxicity
  5. Sudden stretching mid-flower usually indicates inadequate light or far-red penetration through the canopy
